Solar-Powered Terrain Walker

SolVoyager: a Rutgers senior design quadruped combining single-motor leg mechanisms, a lightweight hybrid structure, solar charging, and Arduino control to navigate obstacles while carrying a small payload.

Focus Mechanism design and embedded controls
Tools SolidWorks, ANSYS, Arduino
Prototype Four legs, five motors, and a reported height of 16 inches
Actuation One motor per leg plus one steering motor, versus 18 motors in the earlier six-legged concept

Overview

SolVoyager was developed by Rutgers Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering team ME_B5 for the 2023-2024 senior design sequence. The challenge was to build an autonomous, solar-powered walking vehicle that could carry an 8-ounce payload, negotiate obstacles and inclined terrain, and remain below 18 inches in height. Wheels and tracks were prohibited. A race and engineering expo provided the final demonstration context, while search and rescue motivated the emphasis on compact size, reliability, and operation away from mains power.

The team selected a four-legged architecture with a Chebyshev four-bar mechanism driving a pantograph in each leg. Mechanical geometry creates the walking trajectory, allowing one motor per leg. A fifth motor steers the front leg modules through a separate four-bar linkage. PETG framing, aluminum load-bearing links, ball-bearing joints, and a solar-charged battery complete the system.

Requirements and Concept Selection

  • Walk without wheels or tracks, carry an 8-ounce payload, and change direction autonomously.
  • Stay below an 18-inch height limit; the poster reports a final height of 16 inches.
  • Balance stability, obstacle traversal, speed, power consumption, manufacturability, and a $700 departmental budget.
  • Keep the electronics and payload supported while limiting lateral sway and the risk of tipping during walking and turning.

For steering, the team compared pivoting the front leg modules on a fixed chassis with splitting the chassis around a central hinge. The fixed-body approach was selected to simplify weight distribution and balance. A linkage driven by one steering servo replaced more complex two-motor, pulley, and rack-and-pinion concepts.

Leg Mechanism and Kinematic Analysis

A continuously rotating crank drives the Chebyshev four-bar. Its extended coupler traces a small, inverted walking path, which the pantograph enlarges and flips into a foot trajectory with a nearly flat stance and an elevated return. Each seven-link leg has one degree of freedom. The detailed report and poster describe approximately 270 degrees of crank rotation in stance and a 90-degree quick return, supporting a gait intended to keep three feet in their ground-contact portions at a time.

Position analysis tracked the crank angle through a full revolution, solved the intermediate joint positions, and plotted the foot and coupler paths. The calculated transmission-angle ranges were approximately 83-153 degrees and 80-128 degrees. The first range exceeds the preferred band during part of the cycle; the team checked its behavior in motion simulation rather than treating the geometry as ideal throughout. MotionGen, graphical position calculations, MATLAB, and SolidWorks supported this analysis.

Link dimensions and joint construction

The report's position-analysis dimensions, in inches, are a = 1.25; b1 = 1.875; b2 = 3.75; c = d = f = 1.5625; h = 5.75; m = 0.625; z2 = 1.25; z3 = z6 = 2.75; z4 = 5.5; and z5 = z7 = 2.5. Full equations, joint labels, and motion plots appear on pages 15-19 and in Appendix C.

Three fixed joints support each leg: the motor output, the upper four-bar pivot, and the lower pantograph pivot. Nested inside/outside links distribute load across both sides of moving joints, reducing the bending and bearing movement associated with simple side-by-side links. M3 threads, 4 mm shoulder screws, spacers, and set screws retain the joints. Most moving joints use one bearing; the crank-to-coupler joint retains three bearings because the crank must rotate through a full circle without interference. A 25T servo horn connects the motor to the crank.

Chassis, Steering, and Structural Analysis

The final chassis uses twelve printed PETG H-beams joined with mending plates, brackets, and fasteners. Rear leg modules bolt directly to the frame; front modules mount on vertically aligned pivots. A servo and sliding drive link move the steering four-bar, rotating both front modules while the body remains fixed. The chassis is about 16 inches long and 8.25 inches wide; including the leg bases, the reported width is approximately 12.15 inches.

Vehicle-steering analysis used a 16-inch wheelbase, a nominal 12-inch track, and a 20-inch inner-path design parameter. MATLAB calculations gave an approximately 6-degree front offset for the selected geometry. Because leg timing must remain coordinated for balance, turning also involves foot slip rather than simply assigning different speeds to inner and outer wheels. The report treats this as a practical tuning challenge.

ANSYS static and dynamic analyses guided geometry and material selection. The team compared plastics and wood before using PETG for the frame and four lower-stress links per leg, with 6061 aluminum for the crank, extended coupler, and ground-contact link. Plastic versions of these highly loaded parts showed unacceptable deformation, and geometric enlargement was constrained by the mechanism. Leg studies considered exaggerated loading, including a leg supporting half the robot's weight, while an H-beam study used 40 lbf at each end. These were design simulations, rather than measured load ratings for the finished robot.

Power, Sensors, and Embedded Control

  • Energy: a nominal 20 W, 12 V solar panel and a 7.4 V, 5200 mAh LiPo battery. The report describes a panel controller and buck-converter charging path.
  • Actuation: five continuous-rotation servos rated around 30 kg-cm, or approximately 3 Nm stall torque at 7.4 V; four drive legs and one steers.
  • Control: an Arduino Mega and servo driver coordinate walking and turning; the final assembly also uses a separate 9 V supply for the Arduino.
  • Feedback: external encoders with printed 1:3 gearing provide approximately 6-degree motor-position resolution, compensating for servos purchased without built-in encoders.
  • Autonomy: a TF-Luna ranging sensor provides obstacle-distance input, and a Bluetooth module remotely starts and stops the autonomous routine.
  • Integration: color-coded wiring, a solderable distribution board, and mechanical separation keep wiring clear of the moving leg mechanisms.

The programming work centered on coordinating motor position and gait timing while tuning the PID controller through repeated tests. An Appendix C torque estimate assumed a 15 lb robot, a quarter of its weight on each leg, a 1.2 dynamic load coefficient, and a 0.1397 m lever arm at 45 degrees, giving approximately 1.98 Nm. The nominal 3 Nm servo selection provided margin relative to that calculation; this was a sizing estimate, not a measured torque requirement.

Manufacturing and Assembly

Machined aluminum links

We produced three aluminum parts per leg, twelve in total. Stock was milled to size, mounting and bearing holes were drilled, screw holes were tapped, and bearing bores were finished with a 0.354-inch reamer. Slots were opened through drilling and milling, and edges were rounded to reduce unnecessary mass. Mislocated holes in an initial ground-contact link required rework, illustrating the importance of setup, datum selection, and inspection before repeating a part across all four legs.

PETG printing and modular construction

The final build used PETG despite earlier ABS and wood concepts retained in parts of the report. The four plastic links per leg and the structural H-beams were printed at 70% infill. Motor and encoder mounts and the steering mechanism used 15% infill where loading was lower. Printed mounting holes reduced drilling variation. Leg modules were assembled and checked separately before installation on the frame, with 1 and 2 mm spacers maintaining clearance between links and the base.

Bearing fits and assembly tolerances

The joints use 4 mm bore, 9 mm outside-diameter, 4 mm thick deep-groove bearings. Shoulder screws locate the inner rings, while the rotating outer rings need retention in the links. The report records approximately 4.02 mm bearing bores and 3.98 mm screw shoulders, a 0.04 mm clearance. PETG compliance helped retain bearings in the printed links; Loctite 609 retaining compound addressed fit variation in the machined aluminum bores. Washers and spacers prevented rubbing, while set screws reduced shaft movement.

Testing, Results, and Limitations

Validation began with a fully printed single-leg prototype on a stationary vertical rig. This exposed collisions between links, joints, and bolts before all final parts were manufactured. The team added spacers, adjusted clearances, and fit-checked parts as they became available. Straight edges and calipers checked chassis warping, while obstructing fasteners and nearby components were modified during assembly. Each motor, the Arduino interface, the solar panel, and the battery were tested independently before integration.

The report and poster identify practical next steps: motors with integrated encoders, stronger motor output, fewer software delays in the gait and steering cycles, and protective housings against water and debris. These improvements would reduce assembly effort and improve reliability outside controlled demonstrations.

Standards and engineering practices documented by the team

The report discusses ANSI C18.2M battery specifications, IEEE 1679.1-2017 lithium-battery guidance, NTN bearing-fit recommendations and ISO 286-1, OSHA 1926.963 testing practices, OSHA 1910.132 personal protective equipment, and OSHA 1910.212 machine guarding. These were references for component checks, fit selection, wiring separation, shop PPE, and guarding; the project does not claim independent certification. My machine-guarding contribution checked the milling machine's guard, safety shield, and anchoring.
