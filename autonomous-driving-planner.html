Autonomous Driving Local Planner with Reinforcement Learning

A hierarchical CARLA planning system for highway merges, unprotected left turns, and sudden cut-ins using global A* routing, PPO local trajectory generation, and fixed PID tracking.

Focus Hierarchical planning and closed-loop evaluation
Stack Python, CARLA, PyTorch, Stable-Baselines3
Planner Global A* plus PPO local trajectory generation
Key Result 86% success in 100 highway-merge episodes

Overview

This project focuses on the kinds of traffic interactions where simple reactive rules often break down: contested highway merges, unprotected left turns, and abrupt cut-ins. I built the system so planner changes could be measured in a controlled way without retuning the low-level controller every time.

Implementation

The stack keeps global A* routing and longitudinal and lateral PID tracking fixed, then replaces the local planning layer with a PPO policy. At each simulator step, the learned policy predicts short-horizon trajectory shaping parameters and target speed, letting the ego vehicle react to surrounding traffic while staying aligned with the route-level objective.

I built the training and evaluation pipeline in Python with reproducible scenario generation, observation design for route and traffic interaction cues, reward shaping for safety and progress, and rollout analysis tooling. The action space was designed around trajectory generation rather than direct throttle and steering, which kept the learning problem focused on planning instead of low-level control.

The policy input was organized around three pieces of context: ego and route-tracking state, merge-gap geometry, and neighboring traffic. That structure let the network reason about whether a candidate gap was opening or closing while still preserving the route-following signals needed to stay on a stable local path.

Model Input Structure

The PPO observation combines a 12-dimensional ego and route-tracking state, a 10-dimensional merge-gap description, and per-vehicle traffic features so the policy can time merges without giving up route alignment or short-horizon safety context.

Results

In the tuned highway-merge benchmark, the PPO planner reached an 86% success rate over 100 episodes while outperforming a scripted aggressive baseline on total successes. The project also exposed an honest tradeoff: the learned planner improved completion reliability, but comfort and decisiveness still needed work when measured with jerk-based metrics and long-tail completion times.

What I Built

  • Hierarchical planner combining global A* guidance, PPO local planning, and fixed PID tracking.
  • Reproducible CARLA scenarios for merges, left turns, and sudden cut-ins.
  • Observation and reward design for route adherence, safety, progress, and comfort.
  • Evaluation tooling for success rate, crash rate, completion time, and jerk-based ride quality.

Robustness Under Observation and Action Noise

I also evaluated how the merge planner behaved when moderate observation and action noise were injected at test time. The clean PPO policy held up better than expected: its merge behavior stayed stable, the added noise did not materially break its decision process, and in many runs it actually committed more decisively to an available gap while preserving smoother overall behavior.

The noise-trained PPO policy behaved differently. Rather than learning a more robust version of the same merge strategy, it appeared to learn a less stable one. That result makes sense in this setup because the policy directly controls lateral trajectory anchors and target speed; once noise is present during training, even small perturbations can create larger downstream changes in the generated local path and make the already difficult merge-timing problem harder to solve cleanly.

Success and crash rates under noise show that the clean PPO policy retained a strong merge strategy, while the noise-trained variant gave up reliability instead of gaining robustness.
Completion-time and jerk comparisons suggest that the clean policy remained decisive and comparatively smooth even with perturbations, whereas the noise-trained policy paid a larger performance penalty.

Reward Ablation Study

To understand which reward terms were doing the real work, I removed comfort, lane, and traffic penalties one at a time and compared the resulting success rates and jerk metrics against the full reward function. The ablation runs showed that all three terms matter, but they influence behavior differently: traffic-aware incentives protected success rate most strongly, while comfort and lane terms shaped how aggressively the planner used the available merge space.

Reward Variant Success Rate P90 Max Jerk (m/s^3)
Full Reward Function 83% 590.604
No Comfort Penalty 66% 577.030
No Lane Penalty 62% 592.208
No Traffic Penalty 58% 292.622

Example rollout for the full reward function: watch evaluation video.

The full reward produced the best overall merge reliability. Removing the traffic penalty caused the sharpest collapse in success rate, which reinforces how important explicit interaction awareness was for gap selection. Removing comfort or lane terms also hurt performance, but those variants mostly exposed how much those penalties were stabilizing the local planner's path and merge timing rather than simply lowering jerk on their own.
