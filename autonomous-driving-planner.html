Autonomous Driving Local Planner with Reinforcement Learning

A hierarchical CARLA planning system for highway merges, unprotected left turns, and sudden cut-ins using global A* routing, PPO local trajectory generation, and fixed PID tracking.

Focus Closed-loop planning in highly interactive traffic
Stack Python, CARLA, PyTorch, Stable-Baselines3
Planner Global A* plus PPO local trajectory generation with fixed PID tracking
Key Result 83% merge success and 80% tuned left-turn success

Overview

This project grew into a closed-loop CARLA framework for studying three safety-critical interaction problems: highway merges, unprotected left turns, and sudden cut-ins. The main design choice was to keep the same low-level PID tracker across both classical and learned planners so planning improvements could be measured directly rather than being mixed with controller retuning.

Implementation

The baseline stack used global A* routing, a local waypoint planner, and fixed longitudinal and lateral PID control. On top of that benchmark, I replaced the rule-based local planner with PPO-based short-horizon trajectory generation while preserving the same global route backbone and low-level controller interface. At each simulator step, the learned policy predicted local trajectory-shaping parameters and target speed instead of direct steering and throttle.

The full framework included reproducible scenario generation, baseline behavior profiles, observation design for route and interaction cues, scenario-specific reward shaping, and batch evaluation across success, crash, completion-time, and jerk metrics. Highway merging used merge-gap and nearby-vehicle features, while the unprotected left-turn setup added phase guidance and conflict-zone timing features to support yield-or-commit decisions.

The policy input was organized around three pieces of context: ego and route-tracking state, merge-gap geometry, and neighboring traffic. That structure let the network reason about whether a candidate gap was opening or closing while still preserving the route-following signals needed to stay on a stable local path.

Model Input Structure

The PPO observation combines a 12-dimensional ego and route-tracking state, a 10-dimensional merge-gap description, and per-vehicle traffic features so the policy can time merges without giving up route alignment or short-horizon safety context.

Results

The final project showed that the framework was useful not just for one planner, but for measurable iteration across multiple interactive scenarios. In highway merging, PPO improved from an earlier 76% success-rate policy to an updated 83% success-rate policy while reducing P90 successful completion time from 24.5 s to 15.25 s. In unprotected left turns, reward tuning pushed the phase-guided PPO policy from 44% to 80% success, bringing it close to baseline reliability while producing smoother successful trajectories.

What I Built

  • Shared CARLA benchmark framework for highway merges, unprotected left turns, and sudden cut-ins.
  • Hierarchical planner architecture combining global A* guidance, PPO local planning, and fixed PID tracking.
  • Observation and reward design for merge timing, left-turn commitment, route adherence, safety, and comfort.
  • Evaluation tooling for success rate, crash rate, completion time, jerk, and noise-robustness analysis.

Robustness Under Observation and Action Noise

I also evaluated how the merge planner behaved under degraded observations. In the final project results, the clean PPO policy scored 83% success in the clean environment and 86% success when evaluated in the noisy environment, while the noisy-trained PPO policy dropped to 68% success under the same noisy test.

That result suggests that naive noise injection did not automatically improve robustness. In this setup, the clean policy remained competitive under perturbation, while the noisy-trained policy appeared to destabilize merge timing and local trajectory generation instead of learning a stronger version of the same strategy.

Success and crash rates under noise show that the clean PPO policy retained a strong merge strategy, while the noise-trained variant gave up reliability instead of gaining robustness.
Completion-time and jerk comparisons suggest that the clean policy remained decisive and comparatively smooth even with perturbations, whereas the noise-trained policy paid a larger performance penalty.

Reward Ablation Study

To understand which reward terms were doing the real work, I removed comfort, lane, and traffic penalties one at a time and compared the resulting success rates and jerk metrics against the full reward function. The ablation runs showed that all three terms matter, but they influence behavior differently: traffic-aware incentives protected success rate most strongly, while comfort and lane terms shaped how aggressively the planner used the available merge space.

Reward Variant Success Rate P90 Max Jerk (m/s^3)
Full Reward Function 83% 590.604
No Comfort Penalty 66% 577.030
No Lane Penalty 62% 592.208
No Traffic Penalty 58% 292.622

Example rollout for the full reward function: watch evaluation video.

The full reward produced the best overall merge reliability. Removing the traffic penalty caused the sharpest collapse in success rate, which reinforces how important explicit interaction awareness was for gap selection. Removing comfort or lane terms also hurt performance, but those variants mostly exposed how much those penalties were stabilizing the local planner's path and merge timing rather than simply lowering jerk on their own.
